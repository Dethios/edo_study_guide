# Strategic Acquisition and Capability Integration: An Exhaustive Analysis of ACAT I Programs within NAVWAR and PEO IWS

## 1. Introduction and Strategic Context

The modernization, sustainment, and lethality of the United States Navy rely fundamentally on the successful execution of Major Defense Acquisition Programs (MDAPs). In an era characterized by great power competition and the proliferation of advanced anti-access/area-denial (A2/AD) weapon systems, the Department of the Navy must maintain absolute dominance across the electromagnetic spectrum, the cyber domain, and the traditional maritime theater. To achieve this, the Navy relies heavily on two premier systems commands and their affiliated Program Executive Offices (PEOs): the Naval Information Warfare Systems Command (NAVWAR) and the Program Executive Office for Integrated Warfare Systems (PEO IWS).

NAVWAR, operating through its constituent PEOs—specifically PEO C4I (Command, Control, Communications, Computers, and Intelligence), PEO Digital and Enterprise Services, and PEO MLB (Manpower, Logistics & Business Solutions)—is tasked with delivering the comprehensive information warfare platform from seabed to space. Concurrently, PEO IWS develops, delivers, and sustains operationally dominant combat systems, radars, and effectors that translate tactical data into kinetic and non-kinetic action.

This comprehensive report details the Acquisition Category I (ACAT I) programs managed by NAVWAR and PEO IWS, explicitly identifying the program offices responsible for each capability. Beyond merely cataloging these multi-billion-dollar initiatives, this analysis delves into the second- and third-order implications of their deployment. It examines how data transport and enterprise systems developed by NAVWAR (such as CANES, MIDS, and Navy ERP) serve as the vital connective tissue for the combat and weapon systems developed by PEO IWS (such as AEGIS, CEC, and SM-6). Ultimately, the convergence of these ACAT I programs forms the architectural foundation of the Navy's Distributed Maritime Operations (DMO) and its contribution to the Combined Joint All-Domain Command and Control (CJADC2) construct, most notably manifested through initiatives like Project Overmatch.

## 2. The Defense Acquisition System and the ACAT Framework

To grasp the scale, complexity, and strategic weight of the programs managed by NAVWAR and PEO IWS, it is necessary to establish the exact parameters of the Department of Defense (DoD) acquisition framework. The Defense Acquisition System divides acquisition programs into several Acquisition Categories (ACATs), which dictate the level of statutory oversight, the Milestone Decision Authority (MDA), and the mandatory regulatory documentation required for progression through the acquisition lifecycle.

### 2.1 Major Defense Acquisition Programs (MDAPs)

The ACAT I designation is synonymous with Major Defense Acquisition Programs (MDAPs). By statutory definition, an MDAP is an acquisition program on the Major Capability Acquisition (MCA) pathway that is estimated to require an eventual total expenditure for Research, Development, Test, and Evaluation (RDT&E) of more than $525 million in Fiscal Year (FY) 2020 constant dollars, or an eventual total expenditure for procurement of more than $3.065 billion in FY 2020 constant dollars. To provide context on the budgetary footprint, of the $310.7 billion requested for DoD Investment funding in FY 2025, active MDAPs accounted for $96.1 billion—approximately 31 percent of total funding.

### 2.2 Information Systems and Defense Business Systems Classifications

For highly complex software, networking, and business capability systems—which comprise a significant portion of NAVWAR's portfolio—the DoD utilizes equivalent programmatic categories:

ACAT IAC / IAM: These classifications historically pertained to Major Automated Information Systems (MAIS). "IAM" indicates that OSD retains oversight, while "IAC" indicates that the MDA has been delegated to the component level, specifically the ASN(RD&A) or the Deputy Assistant Secretary of the Navy (DASN).

BCAT I: Business Capability Acquisition Category I programs are Defense Business Systems (DBS) that have massive, enterprise-wide impacts and cross exceptionally high financial thresholds. These systems functionally serve as the business infrastructure equivalent of a kinetic MDAP.

### 2.3 The Transition to Modernized Selected Acquisition Reports (MSAR)

Historically, ACAT I programs were mandated by Title 10 U.S.C. 4351 to submit Selected Acquisition Reports (SARs) to Congress annually in conjunction with the President's Budget, summarizing the latest estimates of cost, schedule, and performance. This statutory requirement terminated at the end of FY 2023, following directives in Section 805 of the FY 2022 National Defense Authorization Act (NDAA) and Section 809 of the FY 2023 NDAA.

The legacy SAR process has been superseded by the Modernized Selected Acquisition Report (MSAR) process. The MSAR framework leverages the Defense Acquisition Visibility Environment (DAVE) and supplemental Excel-based data collection workbooks to provide lawmakers, the Cost Assessment and Program Evaluation (CAPE) office, and the Chief Digital and Artificial Intelligence Office (CDAO) with agile, high-fidelity programmatic data. Consequently, every ACAT I program managed by NAVWAR and PEO IWS is now subject to this modernized reporting cadence, which demands rigorous schedule baseline grouping, unit cost reporting, and detailed histories of significant developments.

## 3. Naval Information Warfare Systems Command (NAVWAR) ACAT I Portfolio

NAVWAR serves as the Navy's premier systems command for acquiring, developing, and sustaining the communication, networking, and intelligence technologies that deliver information warfare capabilities to the fleet. NAVWAR manages a vast and highly complex portfolio through its affiliated Program Executive Offices. PEO C4I alone oversees 126 programs, which includes seven ACAT I programs (two actively acquiring and five in sustainment), 10 ACAT II programs, and 109 programs categorized as ACAT III and below.

The strategic alignment of these offices is critical. For instance, PEO C4I and PMW 101 lead Project Overmatch, the Navy’s specific contribution to the DoD-wide CJADC2 initiative, designed to network forces across all domains. The following table summarizes the ACAT I programs managed under the NAVWAR umbrella, followed by an exhaustive analysis of each program office's contributions and challenges.

### 3.2 PMW 120: Battlespace Awareness & Information Operations

**Program:** Distributed Common Ground System-Navy (DCGS-N)

**ACAT Status:** Increment 1 (ACAT IAC), Increment 2 (ACAT IAM)

PMW 120 focuses on creating and breaking "kill webs" for the warfighter by delivering integrated intelligence, surveillance, reconnaissance (ISR), and electronic warfare capabilities. Its flagship ACAT I program is DCGS-N, the Navy's service component of the overarching DoD DCGS family of systems.

DCGS-N is designed to provide multiservice integration of ISR and targeting capabilities. DCGS-N Increment 1 (ACAT IAC) successfully consolidated various legacy intelligence analytical tools into an integrated computing environment, which has been fielded to force-level ships and key shore sites. DCGS-N Increment 2 (ACAT IAM) represents a generational leap in software architecture, providing robust, cross-domain data fusion and automated analytics workflows. Increment 2 serves as the primary bridge connecting naval operational sensors with the broader Joint and Intelligence Community (IC) enterprises, ensuring that vast quantities of sensor data are rapidly synthesized into actionable intelligence.

Strategic Insights:
The evolution of DCGS-N under PMW 120 highlights a broader trend within NAVWAR ACAT I programs: the migration from hardware-heavy, monolithic deployments to agile, cloud-based software architectures. PMW 120's strategy involves transitioning from sustaining isolated systems to deploying afloat and ashore cloud environments. This software-centric approach allows for more rapid integration of third-party analytical tools and third-order effects, such as the Intelligence Carry-On Program (ICOP), which extends DCGS-N capabilities to smaller, unit-level platforms.

However, this transition introduces significant challenges in software growth. Analytical reviews of PMW 120 and allied PMW 150 software support activities have demonstrated instances of up to 30-percent increases in Source Lines of Code (SLOC) growth, requiring careful cost estimation and rigorous continuous testing. Consequently, PMW 120 heavily utilizes the Navy's Operational Test and Evaluation Force (OPTEVFOR) and maintains stringent cybersecurity oversight to secure Risk Management Framework (RMF) Authorities to Operate (ATOs) in highly classified environments.

### 3.3 PMW 150: Naval Command and Control Systems

PMW 150 is the Navy's preeminent provider of operational and tactical command and control solutions. The office oversees a diverse portfolio that includes two massive ACAT I programs, highlighting the reality that combat efficacy and logistics readiness are operationally inseparable.

#### Program 1: Global Command and Control System-Maritime (GCCS-M)

**ACAT Status:** ACAT IAC

GCCS-M is the Navy's primary C2 program of record and the maritime iteration of the DoD's Joint C2 Family of Systems. It provides near-real-time tactical and operational situational awareness by generating the Common Operational Picture (COP) and Common Tactical Picture (CTP). GCCS-M processes and fuses tracks from ground, air, and maritime sensors, allowing commanders at Maritime Operations Centers (MOCs) ashore and aboard carrier strike groups to synchronize decentralized execution across the battlespace.

Over time, PMW 150's mandate has been to evolve GCCS-M into a more agile, service-oriented architecture (SOA). This evolution involves integrating Maritime Tactical Command and Control (MTC2) software applications, which dynamically plan, direct, monitor, and assess Distributed Maritime Operations (DMO) in real-time. The ultimate goal is to transition from legacy C2 frameworks to cloud-based environments that allow rapid ingestion of Joint Automated Deep Operations Coordination System (JADOCS) data for time-sensitive, dynamic targeting.

#### Program 2: Naval Tactical Command Support System (NTCSS)

**ACAT Status:** ACAT IAC

While GCCS-M commands the tactical fight, NTCSS commands the logistics required to sustain it. Designated as a Mission Essential MAIS (ACAT IAC), NTCSS is a comprehensive suite of software applications providing shipboard and aviation maintenance management, inventory control, and financial business management. Established in 1995 through the merger of legacy systems (SNAP, NALCOMIS, and MRMS), NTCSS is installed on vessels and ashore to support the unit commanding officer in tracking repair work packages, aviation repairables, and critical parts inventory.

Strategic Insights:
The sustainment of the legacy NTCSS ACAT I baseline requires continuous configuration management and integration support. As PMW 150 shifts its focus toward modernized logistics frameworks, such as the Naval Maintenance, Repair, and Overhaul (N-MRO) and Naval Operational Supply System (NOSS) initiatives, maintaining the stability of the NTCSS backbone is paramount to prevent operational degradation and supply chain failures during the transition period.

### 3.4 PMW 160: Tactical Networks

**Program:** Consolidated Afloat Networks and Enterprise Services (CANES)

**ACAT Status:** ACAT IAC / IAM

PMW 160 is tasked with providing mission-effective and cyber-resilient tactical networks to forces afloat. Its premier ACAT I program is CANES, a Chief of Naval Operations-directed initiative that serves as the indispensable core of the Navy's Information Warfare Platform.

CANES was initiated to collapse and replace five disparate legacy network systems (including ISNS, SubLAN, and CENTRIXS-M) into a single, consolidated afloat computing environment. It provides the necessary data transport, edge computing, voice, video, and systems management services across multiple security domains (NIPR, SIPR, SR, etc.) for the fleet. Achieving a Full Deployment Decision (FDD) in October 2015, CANES operates under ASN (RD&A) oversight as an ACAT IAC program.

Strategic Insights:
CANES represents arguably the most foundational enabler of maritime combat power within NAVWAR. Without the hardware and software infrastructure provided by CANES, advanced analytical applications developed by PMW 120 (DCGS-N) and PMW 150 (GCCS-M) simply cannot function afloat. Recognizing the pace of cyber threats, PMW 160 is aggressively driving CANES toward a DevSecOps framework. Operating as the "voice of the application," PMW 160 decouples software development from hardware procurement, ensuring that commercial-off-the-shelf (COTS) baselines are continuously evolved and patched through cyclical Service Pack updates.

### 3.5 PMW 170: Communications & GPS Navigation

**Program:** Navy Multiband Terminal (NMT)

**ACAT Status:** ACAT IC

PMW 170 serves as the premier provider of resilient communications and assured Position, Navigation, and Timing (PNT). The office's flagship ACAT I program is the Navy Multiband Terminal (NMT).

NMT is the Navy's sole protected satellite communications terminal, providing critical, secure, survivable, and interoperable military satellite communications (MILSATCOM). NMT enables high-capacity communication by providing simultaneous access to a variety of existing and future satellite constellations, including Advanced Extremely High Frequency (AEHF), Wideband Global SATCOM (WGS), MILSTAR, Defense Satellite Communications System (DSCS), and the Enhanced Polar System (EPS).

Strategic Insights:
In an era of great power competition, the electromagnetic spectrum is a highly contested domain. NMT is designed specifically to operate in anti-access/area-denial (A2/AD) environments, featuring extreme anti-jam, anti-scintillation, and low-probability-of-intercept/detection (LPI/LPD) capabilities using Extended Data Rate (XDR) waveforms. The strategic importance of this ACAT IC program cannot be overstated; it supports the President's Ballistic Missile Defense priorities and the National Command Authority by ensuring the uninterrupted flow of critical strategic data, even under sustained electronic and physical attack.

### 3.6 PMW 205: Naval Enterprise Networks (PEO Digital)

**Program:** Next Generation Enterprise Network (NGEN) / NGEN-Recompete (NGEN-R)

**ACAT Status:** ACAT I / MAIS

Operating under the Program Executive Office for Digital and Enterprise Services (PEO Digital), PMW 205 manages the Naval Enterprise Network (NEN). The office is responsible for the acquisition lifecycle of enterprise-wide networks, providing secure, seamless, and global computer connectivity for the Department of the Navy ashore.

The cornerstone ACAT I program managed by PMW 205 is the Next Generation Enterprise Network (NGEN) and its subsequent iteration, NGEN-Recompete (NGEN-R). NGEN serves as the successor to the Navy Marine Corps Intranet (NMCI), managing the massive logistical and technical burden of providing end-user hardware, software licensing, and enterprise business services to hundreds of thousands of users.

Strategic Insights:
The magnitude of NGEN-R became acutely apparent during the global shift to remote work environments. PMW 205 demonstrated exceptional resource management by implementing agile contracting strategies during the NGEN-R transition. Leveraging supplemental funding via the Coronavirus Aid Relief and Securities (CARES) Act, PMW 205 accelerated the Navy Enterprise Office 365 migration and sustainment efforts, providing vastly expanded network access and productivity tools despite global hardware shortages and budget constraints. The seamless operation of this ACAT I network is a prerequisite for all shore-based administrative, logistical, and acquisition planning operations.

### 3.7 PMW 790: Shore & Expeditionary Integration

**Program:** Deployable Joint Command and Control (DJC2)

**ACAT Status:** ACAT IAC

PMW 790 delivers resilient, interoperable shore and expeditionary C4I capabilities, ensuring that the infrastructure ashore matches the capacity of deployed fleets. The office's primary ACAT I capability is the Deployable Joint Command and Control (DJC2) system.

Originating as a Secretary of Defense priority transformation initiative, DJC2 provides a standardized, self-contained, rapidly deployable, and scalable joint command and control infrastructure to Geographic Combatant Commands (GCCs) and Combined/Joint Task Force (C/JTF) Commanders. By integrating computer-network-enabled C2 applications with self-powered tactical shelters, DJC2 allows commanders to establish a fully functional operations center in austere expeditionary environments rapidly. PMW 790 integrates this capability with over 60 unique shore and expeditionary networks, including the Shore Tactical Assured C2 (STACC) enterprise, to maintain global reach.

### 3.8 PEO MLB: Manpower, Logistics & Business Solutions

**Program:** Navy Enterprise Resource Planning (Navy ERP)

**ACAT Status:** BCAT I

While strictly categorized as a Business Capability Acquisition Category (BCAT I) program, Navy ERP represents an enterprise IT investment equivalent in scale, complexity, and financial footprint to a traditional kinetic ACAT I weapon system. Managed under PEO MLB (formerly under PEO EIS/Sea Warrior program PMW 220), Navy ERP is the high-velocity back-office system that standardizes the Department of the Navy's business processes for acquisition, financial management, and logistics operations.

Combining commercial off-the-shelf software (primarily SAP architecture) with extensive business process reengineering, Navy ERP utilizes a single database to manage shared common data across functional communities. As of recent reporting, Navy ERP controls over 50 percent of the Navy's financial spending, manages global supply operations, and provides workforce management for over 65,000 personnel. By modernizing its code and upgrading software capabilities, Navy ERP serves as the fundamental enabler for the DoD's strategic goal of reforming business practices for greater performance and affordability, providing the auditability and financial tracking required for every other ACAT I program in the Navy's portfolio.

4. Program Executive Office Integrated Warfare Systems (PEO IWS) ACAT I Portfolio
If NAVWAR provides the nervous system of the fleet through networks and communications, PEO IWS provides the cognitive processing and kinetic muscle. PEO IWS is tasked with developing, delivering, and sustaining operationally dominant integrated combat systems, encompassing surface radars, missiles, launchers, electronic warfare suites, undersea sensors, and integrated shipboard computing environments.

PEO IWS employs a matrixed organizational structure consisting of Integrated Combat Systems (ICS) Major Program Managers (responsible for the overall architecture and hardware refresh of a specific ship class) and Product Line Major Program Managers (responsible for developing specific sensors or weapons across multiple platforms). The total PEO IWS portfolio consists of over 150 programs and projects. Within this massive ecosystem, three primary kinetic and sensor programs are designated as ACAT I (CEC, AMDR, SM-6), alongside the overarching AEGIS Combat System (ACAT 1D). Furthermore, offices like IWS 9.0 (Zumwalt) and IWS 10.0 (SSDS) manage the complex integration of these elements into specific hull forms, operating as pseudo-ACAT I integration environments.

| -------------- | ---------------------- | ------------------- | --------------- |

### 4.2 IWS 1.0: AEGIS Combat Systems

**Program:** AEGIS Combat System / AEGIS Modernization (AMOD)

**ACAT Status:** ACAT 1D

PEO IWS 1.0 is responsible for the in-service and new construction combat systems for all AEGIS Cruisers (CGs) and Destroyers (DDGs). Introduced to the fleet in 1983, AEGIS remains the foundation platform for naval air and missile defense.

The AEGIS Modernization (AMOD) program ensures that the combat system outpaces evolving threats via the Advanced Capability Build (ACB) process. Through the ACB methodology, IWS 1.0 continuously integrates commercial hardware refreshes—specifically the Common Processor System (CPS) and Common Display System (CDS)—with profound software capability expansions. Because of the extreme cost and strategic impact of the DDG 51 Flight III upgrade program, the overarching DDG 51 program was elevated to an ACAT 1D status, placing ultimate decision authority at the highest levels of the DoD. The integration of Ballistic Missile Defense (BMD) profiles alongside traditional anti-air warfare requires IWS 1.0 to manage an incredibly dense system of systems architecture.

### 4.3 IWS 2.0: Above Water Sensors

**Program:** Air and Missile Defense Radar (AMDR) / AN/SPY-6(V)1

**ACAT Status:** ACAT 1D

IWS 2.0 oversees the development and procurement of above-water sensors, including surface search radars, electronic warfare decoy systems, and directed energy weapons (HELIOS). The crown jewel of this portfolio is the Air and Missile Defense Radar (AMDR), officially designated the AN/SPY-6.

Operating as an ACAT 1D program, AMDR represents a generational leap over the legacy AN/SPY-1 radar. Utilizing active electronically scanned array (AESA) technology built upon scalable Radar Modular Assemblies (RMAs), SPY-6 provides unprecedented sensitivity, range, and target discrimination capabilities. It is the mandatory sensor enabling the Flight III DDG 51 class to simultaneously execute integrated air and missile defense (IAMD) against ballistic missiles, low-flying cruise missiles, and advanced aircraft.

Strategic Insights:
The development of AMDR highlights the immense technical risk associated with ACAT 1D sensor programs. Integrating the massive power and cooling requirements of SPY-6 into the DDG hull required extensive ship redesigns. Furthermore, the software integration of AMDR with the Aegis combat system (specifically ACB 20/BMD 6.0) required an unprecedented testing cadence, utilizing the Self Defense Test Ship (SDTS) and shore-based Enterprise Testbeds to demonstrate readiness before deployment.

### 4.4 IWS 3.0: Surface Ship Weapons

**Program:** Standard Missile 6 (SM-6)

**ACAT Status:** ACAT I

IWS 3.0 is tasked with designing, procuring, and maintaining the Navy's surface ship weapons systems. The most advanced effector in this portfolio is the SM-6 (Standard Missile 6), an ACAT I major defense acquisition program.

The SM-6 fundamentally alters surface warfare doctrine. By integrating the legacy SM-2 block airframe with the advanced active radar seeker of the AIM-120 Advanced Medium-Range Air-to-Air Missile (AMRAAM), the SM-6 provides over-the-horizon (OTH) air defense, anti-surface warfare (ASuW), and terminal-phase ballistic missile defense (BMD) capabilities. Because the missile possesses an active seeker, it does not strictly require continuous illumination from the firing ship’s radar in the terminal phase, enabling revolutionary "engage-on-remote" (EoR) tactics that vastly expand the engagement envelope of a strike group.

### 4.5 IWS 6.0: Command & Control

**Program:** Cooperative Engagement Capability (CEC)

**ACAT Status:** ACAT IC

IWS 6.0 manages programs that provide network-centric warfare and joint-tracking capabilities. Its flagship ACAT IC program is the Cooperative Engagement Capability (CEC).

Established under a Mission Needs Statement in 1993, CEC transcends traditional tactical data links (such as Link 16). While standard data links pass post-processed tracks, CEC shares raw, real-time radar measurement data among multiple surface ships and aircraft. This sensor netting system extracts measurement data from all participating units and creates a single, integrated, fire-control-quality composite track picture.

Strategic Insights:
CEC is the ultimate equalizer against radar horizon limitations. If a low-flying, sea-skimming cruise missile is obscured from a destroyer's radar by the earth's curvature, but is visible to an airborne E-2D Advanced Hawkeye, CEC allows the destroyer to "see" the missile on its own Aegis displays exactly as if its own radar had detected it. This networked capability is absolutely paramount for modern battle force air and missile defense.

### 4.6 IWS 9.0 & 10.0: The Integration Hubs

While they do not produce a standalone missile or radar, the integration offices within PEO IWS execute ACAT I-level responsibilities by merging the aforementioned components onto specific hull forms.

IWS 9.0 (Zumwalt Integrated Combat Systems): Procures, integrates, tests, and deploys the Total Ship Computing Environment (TSCE) on the DDG 1000 class. This requires massive non-recurring engineering to fuse sensors and weapons into the unique stealth architecture of the Zumwalt.

IWS 10.0 (Ship Self Defense System - SSDS): Manages the combat management systems for Carriers (CVN) and Amphibious platforms (LHA, LHD, LPD, LSD). SSDS integrates sensors like SEWIP and weapons like RAM and ESSM to provide automated, rapid-reaction defense against anti-ship cruise missiles.

## 5. Strategic Convergence: The Naval Integrated Fire Control-Counter Air (NIFC-CA) Architecture

The true analytical value of reviewing NAVWAR and PEO IWS ACAT I programs simultaneously lies in understanding their profound interdependence. The DoD does not fight with isolated platforms; it fights with integrated "kill webs." The ultimate operational manifestation of this convergence is the Naval Integrated Fire Control-Counter Air (NIFC-CA) system of systems.

NIFC-CA is a non-ACAT project office specifically designed to ensure the perfect alignment of multiple ACAT I pillar programs across different commands. The architecture relies on an intricate sequence of data flow across systems managed by NAVWAR and PEO IWS:

| NIFC-CA Kill Chain Phase | System Utilized     | Managing Office              | Function in the Kill Web                                                                                                         |
| ------------------------ | ------------------- | ---------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 1. Detection             | AMDR (SPY-6)        | PEO IWS 2.0                  | Advanced AESA radar detects an incoming threat well beyond the visual horizon of the fleet.                                      |
| 2. Sensor Netting        | CEC                 | PEO IWS 6.0                  | Fuses raw radar measurement data from AMDR and airborne sensors into a single, fire-control-quality composite track.             |
| 3. Data Transport        | MIDS (TTNT/Link 16) | NAVWAR PMW 101               | Securely distributes the fire-control solution and tactical commands across the battlespace via high-capacity waveforms.         |
| 4. Compute & C2          | CANES & AEGIS       | NAVWAR PMW 160 & PEO IWS 1.0 | The CANES afloat network feeds data into the AEGIS Combat System, which computes the weapon assignment and launch authorization. |
| 5. Kinetic Intercept     | SM-6                | PEO IWS 3.0                  | The ship fires an SM-6, utilizing the missile's active seeker and engage-on-remote capabilities to destroy the threat.           |

This functional matrix illuminates a critical vulnerability in the acquisition lifecycle: a schedule slip, software deficiency, or cybersecurity failure in any single ACAT I program exponentially degrades the lethality of the entire fleet. If NAVWAR's PMW 160 fails to deliver a CANES hardware refresh on time, IWS 1.0 cannot install the latest AEGIS software baseline. If NAVWAR's PMW 101 experiences delays in MIDS JTRS TTNT waveform development, IWS 3.0 cannot fully exploit the over-the-horizon kinematic range of the SM-6. The interoperability of these distinct programs is not a secondary requirement; it is the fundamental premise of naval combat power.

## 6. Acquisition, Testing, and Modernization Challenges

As these ACAT I programs transition from initial procurement to sustainment and modernization, several systemic challenges face NAVWAR and PEO IWS leadership, requiring constant adaptation of the acquisition framework.

### 6.1 Software Growth and Agile Development

The traditional DoD 5000 series acquisition model was designed for linear hardware procurement (e.g., building a physical ship or a missile). However, programs like DCGS-N, CANES, and AEGIS Modernization are fundamentally vast software ecosystems. Analytical reviews of programs under PMW 120 and PMW 150 reveal significant cost growth driven by Source Lines of Code (SLOC) expansion and complex software support activity requirements. To mitigate this, NAVWAR and PEO IWS program offices are rapidly adopting DevSecOps principles and the Software Acquisition Pathway (SWP). Transitioning to containerized, cloud-based architectures (utilizing platforms like Azure IL-5) allows for the rapid fielding of patches and capability increments, bypassing the slow, traditional milestone-based deployment cycle.

### 6.2 Cybersecurity and Obsolescence Mitigation

The necessary reliance on Commercial-Off-The-Shelf (COTS) hardware to keep costs manageable introduces severe supply chain vulnerabilities and rapid obsolescence risks. Systems like CANES require physical hardware refreshes in a cyclical manner (Service Packs) to pace commercial technology. Simultaneously, the cyber vulnerability of tactical networks has forced program offices to heavily integrate cybersecurity measures. PMW 130 acts as the connective tissue in this regard, ensuring strict Risk Management Framework (RMF) compliance across all shipboard and ashore architectures to maintain continuous Authorities to Operate (ATOs).

### 6.3 The Burden of Enterprise-Level Integrated Testing

Because systems like AMDR, AEGIS, and SEWIP are deeply intertwined, independent component testing is entirely insufficient. PEO IWS must fund and coordinate massive Enterprise Testbeds—such as the Surface Combat Systems Center (SCSC) at Wallops Island and the Self Defense Test Ship (SDTS)—to validate combat system integration before fielding to active DDGs and CVNs. Furthermore, oversight entities like the Director, Operational Test and Evaluation (DOT&E) mandate stringent evaluations, including full-ship shock trials and Failure and Recoverability Mode testing, to ensure that the integrated digital and physical architecture can survive battle damage. The financial and logistical burden of this "system of systems" testing continually strains RDT&E budgets but is essential for mission assurance.

## 7. Conclusion

The ACAT I and BCAT I programs managed by NAVWAR and PEO IWS are not merely distinct procurement line items; they are the highly synchronized organs of the United States Navy's warfighting capability. NAVWAR’s portfolio—ranging from the afloat network backbone of CANES and the global situational awareness of GCCS-M, to the resilient satellite links of NMT and the enterprise business infrastructure of Navy ERP—provides the indispensable digital architecture required for modern conflict. Operating on top of this architecture, PEO IWS’s portfolio—featuring the unparalleled radar fidelity of AMDR, the sensor netting of CEC, the kinetic reach of SM-6, and the overarching orchestration of the AEGIS Combat System—translates data into overwhelming lethality.

As the strategic environment demands faster decision cycles and greater distribution of forces, the historical silos between platform, network, and weapon acquisition must continue to dissolve. The shift toward Modernized Selected Acquisition Reports (MSAR) and the alignment of capabilities under the Naval Integrated Fire Control-Counter Air (NIFC-CA) construct reflect an acquisition system adapting to this reality. Ultimately, the future combat efficacy of the United States Navy rests on the successful, synchronized execution of these paramount programs by NAVWAR and PEO IWS.
